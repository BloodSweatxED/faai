# L4 — Consent and Disclosure

<div class="hy-badge hy-2">HY-2 · Medium Yield</div>

---

## Bottom Line

Patients have a right to know when AI is materially involved in their care, the rules are jurisdiction-specific and moving, and the practical playbook is simpler than the legal landscape: disclose plainly, get agreement where required, and never let a patient discover it on their own.

---

## The Concept

Consent law for clinical AI is the least settled part of this section, so this spread separates the stable floor from the moving pieces.

**The stable floor is ethical, not statutory.** Informed consent has always covered the material elements of care, and a recording device in the exam room, an algorithm reading the imaging, or a chatbot answering the portal message can be material to a reasonable patient. The test that travels across every jurisdiction: would this patient be surprised to learn how this was done? Surprise after the fact is the failure state, legally sometimes, relationally always.

**The moving pieces, in three bins.** Recording tools, like ambient scribes, sit in the strictest bin: recording consent laws predate AI, two-party-consent states make unconsented recording independently illegal, and institutional policies almost universally require an explicit ask. Patient-facing generators, like AI-drafted portal replies, sit in the fastest-moving bin: states have begun mandating disclosure when AI generates patient communications, and institutions are adding standing disclosure lines. Clinician-facing tools, like CDS and drafting aids, sit in the quietest bin: generally no individual consent requirement, on the same logic that never required consenting patients to UpToDate, though institutional transparency obligations are growing.

**Disclosure is a skill, not a form.** The ask takes one sentence and lands best when it is concrete and revocable: "I use a program that listens and drafts my note so I can pay attention to you instead of the keyboard. I review everything it writes. Okay if we use it?" Patients overwhelmingly say yes when asked first; framing, agency, and the visible fact that you review the output do the work. Refusals are typically about something specific and manageable, and honoring them cheaply buys durable trust.

## Clinical Anchor

A patient sends a worried portal message about a biopsy result. The reply is warm, thorough, and AI-drafted; the clinician edited it lightly and sent it. Three months later, local news runs a story on the health system's AI messaging pilot, and the patient rereads the thread with new eyes. The warmth now reads as manufactured. She was never told.

Nothing about the message was wrong. The medicine was right, the edit was real, and in her state at the time, no statute was violated. What broke was the surprise: she learned the how from a news crawl instead of from her doctor. The complaint she files is not about accuracy. It says she no longer knows which of her doctor's words were her doctor's. A one-line standing disclosure in the portal would have cost nothing and prevented all of it.

---

## What To Do

1. Know your two governing layers: your state's rules (recording consent, AI-communication disclosure mandates) and your institution's policy. Where they differ, the stricter one is your floor.
2. For ambient scribes: ask every patient, every visit, in plain language, with an easy no. Document the yes the way your policy directs.
3. For AI-drafted patient communications: follow the disclosure mandate if your jurisdiction has one, and consider the standing disclosure line even if it does not. The cost is one sentence; the alternative is the anchor scenario.
4. Run the surprise test on any new tool entering your workflow: if this patient learned exactly how this was done, from someone else, would it cost trust? If yes, you have found a disclosure that should come from you.

---

## How It Fails

Disclosure gets treated as the institution's problem: somewhere in the intake packet, page nine, a paragraph about technology in care. Legally that may even hold. Relationally it fails completely, because consent buried in boilerplate produces exactly the after-the-fact surprise the disclosure existed to prevent, with the added insult that the system can prove the patient was technically told. The trust damage lands on the clinician in the room, not the packet's author. Cost: the patient relationship, plus a complaint file, in exchange for skipping one sentence you could have said.
