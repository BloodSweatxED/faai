# D11 — Owning the Record

<div class="hy-badge hy-2">HY-2 · Medium Yield</div>

---

## Bottom Line

The output is yours the moment you use it. Ownership does not transfer to the vendor, the institution, or the tool, and "the AI wrote it" has never once worked as a defense.

---

## The Concept

D10 covered the moment of signing. This spread covers everything downstream: what the record means, who answers for it, and what to say about how it was made.

**Ownership.** Legally and professionally, the analysis is short. You are the licensed professional. The tool is an instrument. When a chart entry is wrong, the question is never "was the software accurate," it is "did the clinician meet the standard of care," and the standard of care has never included "believed the computer." Vendors reinforce this in writing: every clinical AI contract carries language stating the tool does not practice medicine and the clinician retains responsibility for all decisions. The vendor's lawyers settled the ownership question before you ever logged in. Their answer was you.

**Attribution.** What to document about AI involvement sorts into three bins. Required: whatever your institution's policy and your state's rules say, and increasingly they say something, particularly for ambient scribes and patient-facing tools. Know yours; this book cannot keep up with fifty jurisdictions (see Legal section). Wise: a brief note when AI drafted something consequential and you materially reviewed it, in line with how institutions handle scribe attestation. Noise: disclaiming every spell-check and autocomplete. Over-disclosure buries the disclosures that matter.

**The deposition lens.** The cleanest test for any documentation question: imagine explaining it under oath. Every answer that survives that room has the same shape: "I used a tool, I reviewed its output against the facts, I corrected what needed correcting, and I signed what I verified." Notice the sentence never blames the tool and never hides it. Workflows you would describe differently under oath than to a colleague are workflows to fix now, not after they are discoverable.

---

## Clinical Anchor

The deposition, eighteen months after the shift.

"Doctor, did you review this note before signing it?" There are only two answers. Yes, and the note's errors are your errors, made as a physician, defensible the way physician errors are defensible: against the standard of care, with your reasoning on the record. Or no, and the case is functionally over, because you have just testified that the chart, the legal record of the encounter, is something you attested to without reading.

The follow-up is worse: "Is it your practice to sign AI-generated notes without reading them?" Practice. Not this note; all of them. Every note you signed in that EHR's audit log, with its time-stamps showing four seconds between note generation and signature, is now in scope.

The audit log is the witness clinicians forget. It records exactly how long the review took. Diligence, it turns out, is measurable in retrospect.

---

## What To Do

1. Learn your institution's AI documentation policy and your state's disclosure requirements. If your institution has none, that is worth raising before plaintiff's counsel raises it.
2. Document material AI use the way scribe use is documented: briefly, factually, once. "Note drafted with ambient AI scribe; reviewed and edited by me."
3. Never document a review that did not happen. An attestation contradicted by the audit log is worse than no attestation.
4. Run the deposition lens over your standing workflows once a quarter: could I describe this process under oath without flinching? Fix the ones where the answer is no.

---

## How It Fails

Ownership gets assumed to sit elsewhere: with the vendor, because they built it; with the institution, because they bought it and mandated it; with informatics, because they configured it. Every one of those parties has a document establishing the opposite, and none of them will be named on the chart. The assumption survives because it is never tested in ordinary practice; nothing in the daily workflow contradicts it. The cost is discovering the true answer at the worst possible moment, in front of a lawyer, when the vendor's indemnification clause, the institution's policy, and your signature all point at the same person.
